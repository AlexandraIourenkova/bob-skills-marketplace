# Redesign lenses

Improving a bad process gives a faster bad process. Before proposing anything, look at the process as it is through each lens below and write down what it shows. Then build two options: A improves the process, B replaces it.

## The lenses

1. **Purpose.** State what the process is for in one sentence, from the receiver's side. Then, for every step: does it move the case toward that outcome, or does it serve the process itself (logging, forwarding, re-entering, asking permission)?
2. **Archaeology.** Which steps exist because of a constraint that is gone: a system that could not share data, a fraud twenty years ago, a manager who wanted to see everything? Ask "what happens if nobody does this step?"
3. **Waiting.** Add up the `wait` steps. When waiting is most of the elapsed time, speed of work is not the problem; the hand-offs are.
4. **Hand-offs.** Every change of actor is a queue and a chance to lose context. Which hand-offs exist only to fetch information the first person could have had?
5. **Gather and decide.** Split each human step into finding the facts and making the call. An agent can do the finding and present the evidence; the person keeps the call. This is the most common shape of a good agent.
6. **The trigger.** Could the work start earlier, before anyone asks: when the data arrives, not when someone notices?
7. **The exception path.** Is every case handled like the hardest case? Let the plain cases flow straight through and send people only the exceptions.
8. **Self-service.** Could the person who needs the outcome get it themselves, with the checks built in?
9. **Zero base.** Forget the current steps. Given the outcome, the rules that truly bind and today's tools, what are the fewest steps that would produce it?

## Two options, always

| | Option A: improve | Option B: replace |
|---|---|---|
| What it is | The same steps, the same actors, the slow ones assisted or automated | A different shape from lens 9: fewer steps, other actors, another trigger |
| What it changes | Minutes of work | Elapsed time, hand-offs, who does what |
| What it needs | Usually one integration | Agreement from the people whose step disappears |
| Risk | Low, and so is the ceiling | Higher, and the result is of another order |

Build both as full step lists, so the diagram and the totals can be compared, and state for each: the effect on the number that matters, what can be shown in the weeks available, who has to agree, and what is lost. Something is always lost: a control, a habit, a person's overview. Say what.

## When to bring option B forward

Lead with B, and say why, when one or more of these is true of the process as it is:

- waiting is more than half of the elapsed time;
- more than half of the steps are hand-offs, re-entry or approval;
- the same information is typed or checked more than once;
- a step exists to compensate for a defect in another system;
- the user's own answer to "what is it for" does not need most of the steps.

Lead with A when the rules that bind (law, audit, a system that stays) fix the shape of the process, or when the people whose step disappears are not in the room and cannot be asked in time. Then B goes into the blueprint's roadmap as the next phase.

## A hybrid is a real answer

Often the entry is A in the proof of concept with B drawn as the target: the proof of concept automates the gathering inside the current shape, and the blueprint shows the shape it leads to. Say which one the "after" diagram shows.

## Honesty checks

- An "after" time for a step that does not exist yet is a target or an assumption, never measured.
- A step that still needs a person keeps its person in the diagram. An approval nobody will give up stays.
- When the redesign removes a control, name the control and what replaces it.
